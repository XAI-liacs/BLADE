\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{An operator that knows.}
The failure is represented in the generator's state while it writes,
and a frozen probe ranks failures on new problems without retraining;
only its threshold is problem-specific. As an efficiency tool, however,
it is dominated on our problems by a smoke test, because a failing
evaluation costs seconds and generation dominates the loop. The probe
pays off where one evaluation is expensive and cannot be truncated
(simulations, training runs), and the larger saving may lie in
generation itself: a probe on the partial code could stop a doomed
generation early, which we leave for future work.

\paragraph{An operator that listens selectively.}
The model reads the error trace and the parent code, not the score. AAD
prompts could drop the score of a single parent without loss in one
generation,
and should spend their tokens on what the model acts on: the code to
change and what went wrong with it. Since the counterfactuals are
cheap to run, they are a test that any new prompt design can pass
before it is used in a loop.

\paragraph{An operator with a parameter.}
In one generation the exploration dial moves the measured exploration
of the code and, on every problem we tried, the algorithm families the
model writes. In the loop, selection keeps what scores, so the endpoint
depends on whether the steered design space still contains the family
that solves the problem. On f2 that family is population-based DE, and
the exploit side removes it. Steering towards exploitation narrows the
design space and never helped; steering towards exploration rarely
hurt. When to steer matters more than the direction.
Steering the initial population breaks it (Bragg); anneal and the
stagnation-adaptive dial are the safe settings, and the adaptive dial
is the only arm that widens the search and the best one on MA-BBOB. EC
already has the right tools for such control, such as success rules
and bandits over operators. Our rule saturates because improvements are
rare, so better dose control is an open problem.

\paragraph{Steering versus prompting.}
The prompt moves the measured behaviour more than the dial, but it
narrows the families and imprints its wording on the code; at a fixed
setting neither beats control. Steering is the finer and wording-free
channel, the prompt the stronger one.

\paragraph{The landscape intuition.}
``Exploit on unimodal functions'' describes the run-time behaviour of an
optimiser. The dial changes the designed algorithm, and the code it
writes on the exploit side is single-point local search, which stalls
on an ill-conditioned ellipsoid. An AAD operator acts on the design
space, and the intuitions of parameter control do not carry over to it
directly.

\paragraph{Reading is not writing.}
Three lessons apply to any use of these tools in EC. A direction that
reads a concept well often does not write it (Sec.~\ref{sec:steering}).
The exploration dial transfers to new problems while a probe for the
same quantity does not. And a random direction at the same norm is a
necessary control: a perturbation at every token changes the code
unspecifically, and some of what looks like a steering effect is that.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
We use open-weight models of 8--14B parameters, and the dial on one
model. With 6 to 16 seeds per arm, effects below about 0.05 \aocc are
not resolved, and the gain of the adaptive dial rests on its
pre-registered confirmation. f20 and f9 discriminate little between arms (arm means within 0.24--0.26 on f20, all f9 runs within
0.30--0.38), and the transfer problems have high
failure rates. Concepts are labelled from code patterns. The
non-inferiority margin of 0.10 \aocc is wide, and anneal's schedule at
budget 200 differs from the one at 100. Finally, the sandbox issues we
found change the failure statistics that AAD papers report, including
ours before the fixes.
